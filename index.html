Hi, I'm Sonia!

I’m a computational biologist interested in how genetic variation and cellular context shape the biology of neuropsychiatric disorders. I’m currently a Computational Associate in the Nehme Lab at the Broad Institute of MIT and Harvard, where I study how genetic background shapes molecular and cellular responses to biological perturbations in iPSC-derived neurons and glia. I hold an M.S. in Bioinformatics from Boston University, as well as dual B.A. degrees in Biology (with a specialization in Behavioral Biology) and Psychology, both from BU.

This portfolio showcases a selection of research projects, analyses, and tools I’ve developed as a bioinformatician.

My Work

Nehme Lab, Broad Institute

As the only computational associate in the lab, I lead computational analysis for large, multi-donor iPSC “cell village” experiments, integrating CRISPR/Cas9 editing, immune stimulation, and antipsychotic drug perturbations across neurons and astrocytes using bulk and single-cell RNA-seq. My work spans population-level regulatory mapping (eQTLs, R/QTLs), transcription factor network analysis, and multi-omic integration with chromatin profiling (CUT&Tag). I develop and maintain scalable, reproducible pipelines for alignment, donor deconvolution (Census-seq, Dropulation), and variant-informed cell assignment, along with genomic QC and data infrastructure management. I also created, maintain, and expand our lab’s GitHub. This research has lead to multiple manuscripts and poster presentations.

Cruz-Martín Lab, Boston University (Now University of Colorado)

We wanted to understand whether a highly localized complement perturbation could influence broader cortical circuits. Using in utero electroporation, we overexpressed the schizophrenia-risk gene C4 in ~2% of prefrontal cortex neurons and performed bulk RNA-seq on microdissected tissue to capture circuit-level effects. This approach allowed us to ask whether sparse complement overactivation is sufficient to drive widespread transcriptional changes across largely untransfected cells and reveal mechanisms by which local immune dysregulation might propagate through the cortex.

Undergraduate Honors Thesis

Motivated by the global variability in reported schizophrenia prevalence, I designed a cross-sectional survey to explore cultural and socioeconomic factors that might shape early, prodromal symptoms. The study included participants from Brazil, India, Italy, and the U.S. (N=200 after filtering). Using the PQ-B to assess symptom presence and distress, and the Stereotypes of Schizophrenia Scale to measure attitudes, I examined how prodromal symptoms related to immigration status, substance and cannabis use, income, gender, and age. I also tested the hypothesis that participants from lower-income economies would report higher prodromal symptom rates.
